ONE-PARAGRAPH ABSTRACT (DRAFT)
================================================================================
Foundation-model methodology — large self-supervised pretraining followed
by lightweight downstream adaptation — has driven recent progress in NLP,
vision, and protein structure prediction, and has begun to be applied to
environmental data. We test this paradigm on the policy-relevant problem
of arsenic-risk classification in Bangladesh groundwater. We pretrain
GENESIS, a Transformer encoder, on 2.09 million harmonized global
groundwater chemistry vectors using Masked Geochemical Modeling, at two
sizes (Small: 1.47 M parameters; Base: 9.00 M parameters). We then
benchmark the encoder against XGBoost and L2-regularized logistic
regression on the WHO 10 µg/L exceedance task, using a 224-pair
held-out Bangladesh test set drawn from BWDB monitoring stations
unseen during pretraining. Across every encoder configuration tested
— frozen, end-to-end fine-tuned, randomly-initialized control, and
linear probe — the pretrained Transformer fails to outperform
logistic regression on BD-transfer AUC (LogReg: 0.720 ± 0.012;
best Transformer condition: 0.704 ± 0.028; pretrained-encoder-frozen
configurations: 0.559 ± 0.019, significantly worse with non-overlapping
95 % confidence intervals). The Small encoder transfers better than
the Base encoder, suggesting that pretraining-induced distributional
bias compounds with capacity. We argue that the foundation-model
paradigm, as currently practiced, is mismatched to the data scales,
target structure, and severe regional distribution shift that
characterize environmental-monitoring problems of public-health
interest, and we offer paired-baseline guidelines for future
neural-pretraining work in this space.

SECTION-BY-SECTION OUTLINE
================================================================================

1. INTRODUCTION
   1.1 Bangladesh arsenic crisis: scale, history, monitoring gaps
   1.2 The foundation-model promise (GPT, ESM, V-JEPA, climate FMs)
   1.3 The implicit assumption: scale + transfer ≫ small-data ML
   1.4 Our test: does it hold for groundwater contamination?
   1.5 Contributions:
       - First foundation-model evaluation on hydrogeochemistry transfer
       - Negative result with airtight baselines (XGBoost, LogReg,
         no-pretrain ablation, 2 encoder sizes, 2 transferable targets)
       - Anti-scaling-on-transfer signal: smaller pretrained encoder
         transfers better than larger
       - Cautionary guidance for environmental-ML methodology

2. RELATED WORK
   2.1 Foundation models in scientific domains (ESM-2, AlphaFold, ClimaX,
       Aurora, Pangu-Weather, Prithvi)
   2.2 Groundwater modeling: process-based vs. ML
   2.3 Arsenic prediction in Bangladesh (Podgorski 2020 BD MAR;
       Mukherjee 2020; van Geen 2003)
   2.4 What's missing: a controlled test of pretraining benefit on
       small environmental transfer datasets

3. DATA
   3.1 Pretraining corpus (2.09 M vectors)
       - GEMStat: global stations, ~50 yr coverage
       - USGS NWIS: continental US
       - ADES (France), EEA (Europe)
       - Harmonization pipeline (curation_report.txt)
   3.2 BD held-out (224 temporal pairs from BWDB stations,
       Shamsuddha et al. 2022, never seen during pretraining)
   3.3 Parameter coverage (Table 1)
       - 20 chemistry parameters; 18 auxiliary features (GEE-extracted)
       - BD held-out has As, Fe, Mn, PO4, NO3, pH, Eh, EC, TDS, Ca,
         Mg, Na, K, Cl, HCO3, SO4 — but NO F, U, SiO2, DOC
       - This is a real constraint on BD-transfer benchmarks
   3.4 Temporal-pair construction (final_temporal_pairs.parquet)
       - 3,527 global pairs with shared site, gap = 2-15 yr
       - Wide format, NaN-tolerant
       - Filter to non-NaN target at t1 → As: 1,874 pairs (4.3 % positive)

4. METHODS
   4.1 GENESIS encoder architecture
       - Token sequence: [CLS] + chem + aux + metadata + WBT
       - Variable-length, mask-aware
       - Small: 4 layers, d=128, 4 heads (1.47 M params)
       - Base:  8 layers, d=256, 8 heads (9.00 M params)
   4.2 Masked Geochemical Modeling (MGM)
       - 20 % chem-token masking, no aux/meta masking
       - Per-parameter L2 regression head
       - Stage 1: 100 ep on 2 M vectors
       - Stage 2: 50 ep at higher mask ratio (0.25)
   4.3 Downstream classification setup
       - Binary label: y(t+1) > WHO threshold (As: 10 µg/L; F: 1.5 mg/L;
         NO3: 50 mg/L; U: 30 µg/L)
       - 70/15/15 train/val/test split on global pairs
       - BD held-out: zero-shot transfer eval
   4.4 Conditions (× 4 targets)
       (i)   XGBoost, raw chem + lat/lon (22 features)
       (ii)  L2 logistic regression, median-imputed + standardized
       (iii) GENESIS frozen + 2-layer MLP head (16 K trainable)
       (iv)  GENESIS unfrozen end-to-end (full param count)
       (v)   Random-init "no-pretrain" control + MLP
       (vi)  GENESIS frozen + linear probe (single Linear, 257 trainable)
   4.5 Evaluation
       - 3 seeds per (encoder size × condition × target) cell
       - 200-iteration stratified bootstrap 95 % CIs on AUC, AP, F1_best
       - "Significantly different" = non-overlapping 95 % CIs
       - Cells with single-class train/test splits: skipped, reported as
         data-availability constraint, not as failure of the model

5. RESULTS
   5.1 Headline: BD As-exceedance AUC (Table 2 / Figure 2)
       Methods ranked best-to-worst by BD AUC:
         LogReg               0.720 ± 0.012  (winner)
         Base no-pretrain     0.704 ± 0.028
         XGBoost              0.701 ± 0.033
         Base unfrozen        0.690 ± 0.014
         Base frozen LinProbe 0.571 ± 0.026  (LOSES to LogReg)
         Base frozen MLP      0.559 ± 0.019  (LOSES to LogReg)
   5.2 Anti-scaling on transfer (Figure 3)
       Small frozen BD AUC > Base frozen BD AUC (with non-overlapping CIs
       — confirm after Small sweep finishes)
   5.3 In-distribution global test (Table 3)
       All methods 0.87–0.97 AUC across all 4 targets — encoder isn't
       broken, just doesn't transfer
   5.4 NO3 secondary confirmation (Table 4)
       Same pattern as As: LogReg ties or beats every neural condition
   5.5 F and U: data-availability constraint
       BWDB doesn't measure F or U, so transfer eval undefined.
       Discuss as monitoring-infrastructure caveat
   5.6 Calibration & deployability (Figure 4)
       At F1_best threshold (~0.30), LogReg recalls 93 % of unsafe BD
       wells with 42 % precision — a deployable risk-screening tool
       achievable from raw chemistry without any neural pretraining

6. WHY DID PRETRAINING HURT?
   6.1 Distribution shift quantification (PCA / OT distance between
       pretrain corpus and BD held-out)
   6.2 Pretraining-induced specialization: encoder learns to reconstruct
       global-chemistry distribution, locking in priors that hurt regional
       transfer
   6.3 Linear-additive structure of the As exceedance signal: examined
       via LogReg coefficients (Fe, pH/Eh, HCO3 dominate — all in Smith &
       Polizzotto's 2017 "predictable" subset)
   6.4 Capacity / data-ratio: 9 M params, 1.3 K labeled training samples
       → 7,000 params per labeled positive, severe over-parameterization
   6.5 Why fine-tuning partly rescues but doesn't win (recover 0.56 → 0.69
       BD AUC, never beats 0.72 LogReg): unfrozen optimizer can flatten
       the harmful priors but not erase the inductive bias from MGM

7. WHAT WOULD HAVE TO CHANGE FOR FOUNDATION MODELS TO HELP HERE?
   7.1 Larger labeled BD set (currently n=224 transfer; would need ~10 K)
   7.2 Region-aware pretraining (overweight S/SE Asia in MGM corpus)
   7.3 Different self-supervision objective (causal time-series; not
       static masking)
   7.4 Targets where non-linear interactions matter more (As is dominated
       by linear redox + carbonate proxies)

8. CONCLUSION
   8.1 We pretrained a 9 M-parameter transformer at the data scale most
       environmental labs can afford (2 M vectors), and it was worse than
       logistic regression on the policy-relevant task.
   8.2 Smaller pretrained encoder transferred better — capacity hurt.
   8.3 The cure is not "scale up." It's:
       a) be honest about your transfer baseline;
       b) use the simplest model that fits your data scale;
       c) report negative results.
   8.4 We release code, checkpoints, and the harmonization pipeline so
       others can audit, reproduce, and refute.
